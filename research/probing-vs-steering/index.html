Probing vs. steering in LLM hidden states:

Do Probing and Steering Favor the Same Directions Within and Across Language Models?

In easy words

Inside a language model, a concept like “ice cream is delicious” shows up as a direction in the model’s internal numbers. The direction that is best for detecting the concept is usually not the one that is best for changing what the model says.

There we showed a way to carry a concept from one LLM to another, so what you find in one model can be reused in the next, and measured when that works and when it doesn’t. We also used the same kind of concept direction to make LLMs say “I don’t know” instead of guessing, which turns far more of the uncertain wrong answers into “I don’t know” (18–44 points more abstention) than of the confident ones (2–5 points), at a small cost on answers that were correct (about 1–7 points).

TL;DR

A direction in a language model's hidden state can be used two ways: read it to detect a concept (a probe), or add it to the hidden state to push the model toward the concept (steering). Probe directions are often reused for steering. We ask whether the best probe is also the best steering direction.

We build a one-parameter family of directions that runs from a covariance-adjusted discriminant to the mean difference, and measure both jobs along it at a fixed side effect, in LLaMA, Qwen and Gemma. For truthfulness and sentiment, the best probe is not the best steering direction in any of the six main model–concept combinations (59 of 60 layers in the sweep): steering favors directions closer to the mean difference.

The two roles also travel differently between models. A probe carried through a linear map keeps 81–91% of the target's above-chance probing score; the carried mean difference keeps less of its steering effect in 11 of 12 model pairs. Steering toward “unknown” on PopQA raises abstention by 18–44 points on uncertain errors and by 2–5 points on confident ones.

1One direction, two jobs

A language model turns a text into a hidden state \(h\), a vector of several thousand numbers. A direction \(w\) in that space can do two things.

  • Probing reads it. The score \(w^\top h\) tells you whether a concept, such as a true answer or a positive review, is present in the text.
  • Steering writes along it. Replace \(h\) by \(h + a\|h\|\,w\) and the model's behavior moves toward the concept, with no change to the weights.

Steering methods often reuse a probe direction for the push. That is safe only if the direction that detects a concept best is also the one that changes the model most, and nothing guarantees this. A direction can predict a concept well because it exploits variation that happens to correlate with it, without being the direction along which the model is easiest to move.

The two ends of the family

Write \(\mu_1\) and \(\mu_0\) for the average hidden state of texts with and without the concept, and \(\Sigma_W\) for the pooled within-class covariance. Two directions matter:

  • the mean difference \(\Delta\mu=\mu_1-\mu_0\), which ignores how much each class spreads. It is the steering vector of contrastive activation addition and the refusal direction;
  • the Fisher discriminant \(\Sigma_W^{-1}\Delta\mu\), which corrects \(\Delta\mu\) for within-class spread. Under a shared-covariance Gaussian model it is the Bayes-optimal probe.

Because \(\Sigma_W\) is estimated from fewer examples than there are dimensions, it is singular, so the paper uses a regularized family that connects the two:

\[ w_\lambda \;=\; \frac{(\Sigma_W+\lambda I)^{-1}\,\Delta\mu}{\bigl\|(\Sigma_W+\lambda I)^{-1}\,\Delta\mu\bigr\|} \]

Small \(\lambda\) gives the probe end of the family; as \(\lambda\to\infty\) the direction becomes \(\Delta\mu\). We write \(\lambda\) in units of \(\tau=\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_W)/d\), the average within-class variance per dimension, because raw values are not comparable across models.

Comparing at a fixed side effect

A harder push changes more than the target concept, so a direction could look good for steering just because it disturbs the model more. The paper therefore reads every direction's steering effect at the same side effect \(K\): the average KL divergence between the model's next-token distributions with and without the push, measured on 200 neutral WikiText-2 sentences. The budget \(\kappa_0\) is the \(K\) that \(\Delta\mu\) produces at a fixed strength, set separately for each model and concept.

When the noise leans toward \(\Delta\mu\), the classes overlap along \(\Delta\mu\) and the score \(\Delta\mu^\top h\) separates them poorly. The covariance-adjusted direction tilts away from \(\Delta\mu\) to avoid that spread, and probes better. But \(\Delta\mu\) is by definition the direction in which the class averages differ, so it is also the direction in which a push moves \(h\) the farthest toward the other class. With round noise (stretch 1) the two ends coincide.

The toy is deliberately simple. Here the covariance is known exactly, so the best probe sits at the small-\(\lambda\) end. In real models \(\Sigma_W\) is estimated from fewer examples than there are dimensions, which is why the best probe sits near \(\lambda=\tau\) instead. The budget also lets the probe end push 1.3–1.5 times as hard as \(\Delta\mu\) (from the paper's appendix on the first-order prediction), and for sentiment some intermediate directions beat \(\Delta\mu\), which this toy cannot show. The next section uses measured numbers.

3What happens in real models

The paper tests three instruction-tuned models, LLaMA-3.1-8B-Instruct, Qwen3.5-9B and Gemma-3-12B-it, on two concepts: truthfulness (TruthfulQA answers labeled true or false) and sentiment (2,000 Yelp reviews). The main analysis reads each model at about 0.44 of its depth: layer 14 of 32, 14 of 32 and 21 of 48. For each model and concept it computes \(w_\lambda\) at 13 values of \(\lambda/\tau\), spaced by half a decade from \(10^{-3}\) to \(10^{3}\), plus \(\Delta\mu\) itself. Probing is scored by held-out AUROC. Steering is scored by the change in the answer margin (truthfulness) or in the log-odds of positive versus negative words (sentiment), at the budget \(\kappa_0\).

Result 1: the two roles peak in different places

In all six model–concept combinations, probing is best near \(\lambda=\tau\), with held-out AUROC 0.96–0.98. It drops toward \(\Delta\mu\) (0.60–0.89 there). Steering shows the opposite trend: the strongest directions lie between \(10\tau\) and \(316\tau\). The “plateau” of a curve is the set of directions within one standard error of its best value, and the steering plateau lies to the right of the probing plateau in all six cases.

The matched-text control has no \(10\tau\) or \(100\tau\) points.

The size of the split depends on the concept.

  • Truthfulness. The best probe is a weak steering direction: at \(\lambda=\tau\) it changes the answer margin only 21–64% as much as \(\Delta\mu\) does at the same side effect. No direction on the path steers clearly better than \(\Delta\mu\); the largest gain, 9.6% in Qwen, falls just short of the 10% required in advance, so H1b fails in all three models.
  • Sentiment. The best probe (at \(3\tau\) in LLaMA and Qwen, at \(\tau\) in Gemma) already steers about as well as \(\Delta\mu\), 0.99–1.11 of its effect. Here H1b holds in all three models: a \(\lambda^*\) chosen on one held-out split gives 27–56% more effect than \(\Delta\mu\) on the other.

A first-order picture explains the shape. If the behavior responds only to the component of the push along \(\Delta\mu\), the effect at a fixed side effect is \(S(\lambda)/S(\Delta\mu)\approx \dfrac{a_\lambda(\kappa_0)}{a_{\Delta\mu}(\kappa_0)}\cos(w_\lambda,\Delta\mu)\). It reproduces the position of the steering plateau in all six cases (correlation 0.91–1.00 over the 14 directions). The probe end steers weakly because its cosine with \(\Delta\mu\) is only 0.25–0.46. (This analysis is in the appendix of the current manuscript; it is not in the workshop submission PDF.)

Gemma shows the split most clearly. Its mean difference hardly separates the classes (AUROC 0.596 for truthfulness, 0.631 for sentiment), yet steering along it still changes the model's behavior. A direction can be nearly useless as a probe and still steer.

The robust result is the split, not the exact optimum. Switch the widget to “matched texts”. In the main runs the probe reads different texts than the steering prompts. With one matched template for both, H1a still holds in all six combinations, but the sentiment gain of H1b disappears in LLaMA and Gemma (ratios 1.03 and 0.99) and shrinks in Qwen (1.31 instead of 1.56). Part of the intermediate direction's advantage came from the probe reading full reviews. The matched control also changes the instruction and separator for truthfulness, and shortens the text for sentiment, so it does not isolate one cause.

Result 2: does it hold at other layers?

The layer was chosen in advance. A sweep over ten depths repeats the measurements with a coarser, six-member family (\(0.001\tau, 0.1\tau, \tau, 10\tau, 100\tau, \Delta\mu\)). H1a, that the steering plateau ends further toward \(\Delta\mu\) than the probing plateau, holds in 59 of the 60 combinations of layer, model and concept. The one exception is truthfulness in LLaMA at layer 4, where \(\Delta\mu\) does not steer at all and both plateaus are \(\{\tau\}\). The stronger claim H1b is less stable: for truthfulness it fails at the layers used in the paper but holds at some others; for sentiment it holds wherever \(\Delta\mu\) steers.

Other steering operator. Spherical Steering rotates the hidden state toward the direction instead of adding it. Repeating the measurements with a gated rotation, H1a holds wherever the rotation reaches the same side-effect budget: truthfulness in all three models, and sentiment in Gemma. For sentiment in LLaMA and Qwen the gate stays mostly closed and the rotation never reaches the budget, so the comparison is not defined there. The H1b results under rotation mix the geometry with the gate, so the paper does not read them on their own.

4Do models agree on how a concept is read or changed?

Recent work reports that independently trained models converge on similar representations. Whether two models agree on a concept may depend on whether it is read or changed, and the two roles need different maps to move between models.

Fit an affine map \(h_B\approx M_{A\to B}h_A+b\) by ridge regression on neutral text (12,800 texts, no concept labels). A steering direction is a displacement, so it goes forward through the map. A probe is a linear functional of the hidden state, so it goes through the transpose of the reverse map:

\[ \underbrace{\delta_B=M_{A\to B}\,\delta}_{\text{steering law}} \qquad\qquad \underbrace{w_{A\to B}=M_{B\to A}^{\top}\,w_A}_{\text{probe law}} \]

To compare, each carried direction is scored against the target model's own direction:

\[ \rho_{\text{probe}}=\frac{R_B(M_{B\to A}^{\top}w_{A})-\tfrac12}{R_B(w_{B})-\tfrac12},\qquad \rho_{\text{steer}}=\frac{S_B(M_{A\to B}\Delta\mu_A)}{S_B(\Delta\mu_B)} \]

\(R_B\) is held-out AUROC in model \(B\) and \(S_B\) the steering effect in \(B\) at its own budget. A value of 1 means as good as the target's own direction. The thresholds were fixed in advance: at least 0.8 counts as shared, below 0.5 as not shared.

With each role carried under its own law, a carried probe keeps 81–91% of the target probe's above-chance AUROC in all twelve pairs. The carried mean difference reaches 44–93% of the target's own steering effect, which is below \(\rho_{\text{probe}}\) in 11 of 12 pairs. By the categories fixed in advance, one pair is probe-shared, two sentiment pairs (LLaMA→Qwen and Gemma→Qwen) are both-shared, and nine are inconclusive, mostly because \(\rho_{\text{steer}}\) falls between 0.5 and 0.8. The paper reads this as a tendency in three models, not a clean classification.

The law matters. Switch the probe to the steering law in the widget: probing breaks down in every pair into Gemma (0.10, 0.10, −0.10 and 0.17). An orthogonal Procrustes map, fitted on the same text, probes about as well as the probe law in 10 of the 12 pairs, so the loss comes from applying the forward map to a probe, not from fitting maps on neutral text. Across the twelve pairs, a mean difference carried by the probe law keeps how well it probed in the source (\(r=0.98\)), while one carried by the steering law follows how well the target's own mean difference probes (\(r=0.95\)). The probe law keeps what a direction reads; the steering law keeps where it pushes.

Three caveats the paper states. (1) The maps fit the concept texts less well than neutral text (\(R^2\) 0.13–0.47 against 0.48–0.70), so part of the lost steering effect may come from the map, and no pair can be called “neither shared”. (2) AUROC saturates near 1. On the separation scale \(d'\), the carried probe keeps 65–79% and exceeds \(\rho_{\text{steer}}\) in 9 of 12 pairs instead of 11 (current manuscript; not in the workshop submission PDF). (3) The twelve pairs share models, so “11 of 12” describes a pattern in three models, not twelve independent confirmations.

5Application: steering toward “unknown”

As a use of the same distinction, the paper asks whether the model can be steered to say “I don't know” when it should. For each of 4,000 PopQA questions, with abstention allowed, each model gives one greedy answer and five sampled answers. The questions are sorted by those answers:

The models abstain on 57–78% of the questions and answer 11–21% correctly. The labels describe whether a correct answer is reachable under this sampling, not whether the model knows the fact. C1 is small and conservative, so its intervals are wide.

Steering along \(\Delta\mu\) turns many uncertain errors into abstentions (C2: +44, +30 and +18 points for LLaMA, Qwen and Gemma) but few confident ones (C3: +5, +4 and +2), at a small cost on correct answers (C0: +7, +3 and +1). That holds in all three models. A random direction at the same side effect raises abstention on C2 by only 7, 9 and 1 points, but pushed harder it also moves C2 far more than C3, so uncertain answers give way to any push. What \(\Delta\mu\) adds is more abstentions at the same side effect.

Steering rarely corrects errors. Some knowledge-available errors become correct (11, 3 and 3%), but a random direction produces about as many (6, 6 and 9%). The paper reads these as small pushes on answers that were nearly tied, not as retrieving the fact.

Gating, steering only the questions the probe flags, lowers the cost on correct answers in LLaMA and Qwen (C0: +2 instead of +7, and +0.8 instead of +3) and keeps 65 and 84% of the gain on uncertain errors. By the rule fixed in advance it passes only in Qwen. Across models, a probe for “unknown” carried to another model keeps 89–96% of the target's above-chance AUROC, while a carried \(\Delta\mu\) reaches 30–79% of the target's abstention gain on uncertain errors.

6Limits

The paper lists these limitations; they bound how far the results should be read.

  • Scope. Three concepts (truthfulness, sentiment, “unknown”) and three instruction-tuned models, with the main analyses at one layer per model. The steering of the PopQA experiment was run at one layer only.
  • Not independent. The twelve model pairs share models. Gemma's poorly probing mean difference affects every pair into or out of Gemma.
  • Single directions, two operators. One direction at a time from one family, applied by addition or by gated rotation. Subspaces, nonlinear probes and sparse-autoencoder features are not studied.
  • What is measured. Steering is the change of an answer margin or of word log-odds, not task accuracy. The side effect \(K\) is measured on neutral text, so equal \(K\) need not mean equal disruption of the task.
  • Maps. Fitted on neutral text, they fit the concept texts less well. The within-class covariance is estimated from fewer examples than dimensions, so the small-\(\lambda\) end is the component of \(\Delta\mu\) outside the span of the within-class deviations rather than the exact Fisher discriminant.
  • Truthfulness data. One dataset, with directions fitted and evaluated on the same distribution, so an intermediate direction's gain cannot be told apart from a gain of fitting that distribution.

What this paper adds

  • The direction itself is varied, not just the strength. Earlier comparisons put a trained probe against the mean difference, two fixed directions, or vary the push strength along one direction. This paper moves along the whole family between them and measures probing and steering at every point at a fixed side effect. Result: in all six main model–concept combinations the best probe is not the best steering direction, and in 59 of 60 layers in the sweep.
  • The two roles are compared under their own transfer laws. A steering direction is a displacement and goes forward through the map, \(M_{A\to B}\,\delta\); a probe is a linear functional and goes through the transpose of the reverse map, \(M_{B\to A}^{\top}w\). The laws themselves are those of Chen et al. (2025), who train the maps jointly; here the maps are fitted separately in closed form. What is new is the comparison: the same family of directions is carried in both roles between the same three model families, in every ordered pair, each role under its own law and measured against the target's own direction at a fixed side effect, with the wrong law and an orthogonal map as controls. Applying the wrong law breaks probing into Gemma entirely.
  • Models agree more on how a concept is read than on how it is changed. A carried probe keeps 81–91% of the target's above-chance probing in all 12 pairs; a carried steering direction keeps 44–93% of the target's steering effect, less than the probe in 11 of 12. The probe law preserves what a direction reads (\(r=0.98\) with the source); the steering law preserves where it pushes.
  • Steering toward “unknown” acts on the errors the model was unsure about. On PopQA it raises abstention by 18–44 points on uncertain errors and only 2–5 on confident ones, at a cost of 1–7 points on correct answers, and rarely fixes an answer. A hallucination can be turned into an “I don't know”, mostly where the model already wavered.
  • A closed-form direction between the probe and the mean difference can steer harder. In the main sentiment setting an intermediate direction gives a 27–56% stronger steering effect than the mean difference at the same side effect, though this advantage depends on the setting.

Probe directions should not be reused for steering without checking how well they steer, and should not be copied between models with the steering law.
